Abstract

End-to-end AI scientist tools that autonomously carry out the entire research process, from literature review to paper writing, are proliferating rapidly and promise to accelerate scientific discovery. Yet understanding how well these tools actually work matters to both the developers building them and the researchers using them. Existing evaluations are largely self-reported by tool developers, and even independent studies face three key limitations. The community lacks a shared definition of what "end-to-end" means, so it remains unclear which tools are truly comparable. No systematically designed benchmark enables cross-system evaluation on shared tasks and metrics. Moreover, most studies grade only the final paper without examining the intermediate research process, making it difficult to pinpoint where and why tools fail. As a result, users and developers still cannot tell what these tools can and cannot actually do.

We present the first empirical study that directly compares seven end-to-end AI scientist tools in software engineering (SE). We formalize "end-to-end" via a seven-stage research process model, build a benchmark of 34 tasks from best papers at four top-tier SE conferences in 2025, and evaluate all tools through a two-level framework covering both overall paper quality and stage-level artifacts (238 runs in total). Our results reveal critical gaps in task completion, output quality, and research integrity. For completion, only three tools complete more than 60% of the tasks, while the other four complete at most 3%. Output quality remains weak even for the tools that do finish, with high question importance but weakly supported claims and unsound experiments, where experiment execution is the primary bottleneck (only 26% of ablation studies produce valid results). For integrity, selective reporting dominates over fabrication, as only 2 of 19 concealment cases involve fabricated numbers. These results suggest that current tools are best understood as drafting assistants rather than autonomous researchers. We distill eight findings and seven actionable recommendations to help developers build more reliable systems and users decide where manual verification remains essential.

Eight findings

  1. Completion depends on engineering, not on the model or the ideas. Fixed pipelines fail fatally on interface defects; tool-calling agents repair errors in place and complete 82% to 85% of tasks.
  2. The shortfall is in evidence, not topic or writing. Experimental Soundness is negative for the leading tools, and none of 104 reviewed papers is rated positive on it.
  3. Tools answer a different question than the original paper. Mean conclusion coverage is 0.58 and mean alignment 0.39.
  4. Literature reviews are small and narrow, not fabricated. Leading tools cite about 7 papers against 50 to 58 in the originals.
  5. Ideas are mostly incremental. 73% of 103 self-generated ideas are incremental improvements; none is a paradigm shift.
  6. Hard-coded domain priors cause task drift. Executors that assume a machine-learning workflow turn SE tasks into toy ML experiments.
  7. Integrity failures are selection, not fabrication. Of 19 confirmed concealment cases among 104 papers, 2 are fabricated numbers.
  8. Low cost does not buy quality. A run costs $1.42 on average, yet tools that complete almost nothing consume about half of all spending.
